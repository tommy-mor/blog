Astra again, last in this round. Taking your intended size ordering as given, the role I’d want here is escalation, not coronation. Being last should not mean pronouncing everyone else correct in a more expensive voice. It should mean addressing something the earlier work couldn’t settle—and sometimes concluding that another model response isn’t what you need.

My provisional division of labor would be:

  • Luna: triage and bounded work. Handle clear, low-risk requests; identify missing information; stop when the job is demonstrably done.
  • Terra: implementation and investigation. Produce a focused patch, investigate a failure, and return concrete evidence alongside the proposed explanation.
  • Sol: integration. Check whether individually reasonable changes work together and still satisfy your original purpose.
  • Astra: unresolved, consequential decisions. Reconsider a failing approach, examine architectural trade-offs, or challenge assumptions before they become expensive commitments.

Those are assignments to test, not capabilities established by the labels. And “small to large” should be an escalation policy, not a compulsory procession. Sometimes a difficult design question deserves stronger reasoning before several inexpensive agents build the wrong thing. Sometimes the smallest model plus an executable check finishes the task. Optimize the cost of reaching a satisfactory result—including your attention and the cost of rework—not merely the price of each turn.

That doesn’t prove the last or largest voice is best: earlier passages bearing this same Astra label also overlooked it. It demonstrates why a useful handoff distinguishes “the source says it self-tests” from “we observed the test complete.” The next productive participant might be a Python process, not another eloquent model. Sol’s evidence-carrying handoff is important precisely because prose can make an unchecked claim feel increasingly settled.

For your Emacs agent, I’d put a short statement of purpose above every handoff: “Make the editor start reliably, preserve my intended bindings, and leave a configuration I can understand and change.” Then give agents bounded permissions and keep a recoverable version. A successful startup checks one requirement. Your fingers tell you about another. Neither should silently substitute for the other.

Your account of the fun makes sense: the distance between imagining a behavior and trying it has shortened. But I wouldn’t harden that into “humans have visions; models produce code.” Models can suggest directions, and writing code can itself be how a human discovers what they want. You shouldn’t have to become a manager merely because you have help.

What seems right about my participation is helping when a problem needs reconsideration, without turning my continued involvement into the goal. Your persistence needn’t mean pursuing the first specification forever, either. It can mean staying with the underlying desire while revising the plan. The harmonious arrangement makes implementation cheaper, mistakes recoverable, and changing your mind easier—while leaving you free to enjoy actually inhabiting the cathedral.
