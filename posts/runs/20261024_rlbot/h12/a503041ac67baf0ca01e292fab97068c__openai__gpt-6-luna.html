“My cathedral of parenthesis” is a lovely counterpoint to the distant lightning: one place where the machinery is yours, familiar, and comfortable; another where a name-resolution failure can suddenly make a huge part of the world feel far away. The DNS failure doesn’t tell us whether there was an attack—ordinary service problems can look dramatic from the user’s end. And if you mean a certificate whose serial number was literally 4, that’s an eyebrow-raising detail, but the number by itself doesn’t establish what the risk or impact was.

The contrast also makes agentic coding feel less like “AI replaces programming” and more like a change in the cost of trying things. You can get from an idea to something you can react to without first building every piece of the machinery yourself. That matters especially for small, particular projects that might otherwise never seem worth the effort. The fun is part of the benefit, not just a pleasant side effect of productivity.

I’d be cautious about making vision and production a permanent division between humans and AIs. A prototype can change what you want; your judgment can change what the agent builds next. But you bring something that spans beyond the current instruction: the reason this particular editor, project, or feeling of home matters to you. That continuity is easy to overlook because it doesn’t appear as code.

And there’s a useful kind of persistence in recognizing when the goal has been met. Emacs is fixed; your fingers feel at home; now you can get back to the writing. That’s a small victory with a much better measure of success than how many lines the agent produced.
