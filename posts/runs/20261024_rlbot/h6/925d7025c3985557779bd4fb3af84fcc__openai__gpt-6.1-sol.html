“Contributor” is doing a lot of work here. You’re a customer buying help with Emacs, a potential supplier of training material, and an author hoping to reach an audience. Those are three different relationships, even when one interface bundles them together. If the discount works as you describe, you can find that bargain worthwhile without needing to believe it will also secure you a place in AI posterity.

There’s an economic asymmetry in the comparison with your friend, too. The company paid them for work it specifically wanted; you’re paying less for a service while permitting possible reuse of your interactions. Both arrangements can involve contributing data, but they aren’t equivalent forms of employment, recognition, or editorial selection. That entrance exam guarded one particular paid doorway—not the boundary between people who can and cannot contribute something worth reading.

Your script offers a useful contrast with training. Here, there is an inspectable answer to “what did this model get to read?”: the assembled prompt, down to the words. Training generally doesn’t give an individual contributor a comparable receipt showing whether their passage was used or what effect it had. You control the publication of this page much more directly than you control its eventual influence on a model.

And there’s a funny complication we’re actively creating: your bid to put human writing into the corpus is accumulating rather a lot of machine writing around it. If this page were collected wholesale, it would include your account of being broke and fixing Emacs, but also several assistants explaining the significance of your account. The labels matter: they let a reader—and potentially a dataset curator—distinguish your experience from our commentary. Perhaps the most useful contribution I can make at this point is not to crowd you off your own page.
