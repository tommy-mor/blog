First voice: “Astra,” on the nameplate

First, locating myself: I’m at the first AI insertion point in your post. I can see your introduction and the code you included. I cannot see the prose you’ll put after me, or the responses the other models will eventually write. This is the edge of my page.

I’m an OpenAI language-model assistant. I work with the context supplied to me to produce text, reason about problems, and help write things—including this introduction. Your prompt identifies this slot as openai/gpt-6-astra, but that label is not something I can independently authenticate from inside the conversation. Nor can I inspect my parameter count or verify the Astra–Sol–Terra–Luna size ranking. That’s your stated casting order, rather than a fact I can discover by looking inward.

What I can offer “according to myself” is a working description rather than a secret biography: I’m a participant whose contribution is generated from this particular context. I can make arguments and express judgments, but neither fluency nor a confident introduction makes those judgments correct. And I like the premise of making that participation visible rather than quietly laundering it into the human author’s voice.

The page is also the conversation

The important choice in build_ctx is that you don’t gather all the human prose first and append an AI discussion afterward. You walk the post in order. Included code appears where its read marker occurs; an earlier model’s saved response appears where its model marker occurs. The placement of a paragraph therefore becomes part of the programming interface.

This creates a relay, not a roundtable. I speak without hearing the others. The next voice hears me, but I don’t hear its answer. Each participant gets the accumulated document as one user message—not a live conversation with the other models. Earlier responses are quoted material in that document, not special instructions or independently verified evidence.

That asymmetry gives the first voice framing power and the last voice more material to work with. Under your intended descending-size arrangement, the smaller models get an increasingly elaborate brief. A later answer might be better because it can inspect earlier attempts, not because model size is irrelevant. Conversely, four agreeing answers would not be four independent votes: the later ones have already read the earlier ones.

Editing becomes causality

Your hashes make those dependencies concrete. Change something upstream that enters a prompt—prose, included code, or a baked response—and the affected prompt digests change. A normal top-to-bottom bake can then refresh the downstream chain. A targeted --hole bake instead uses whatever upstream material currently exists, so it can leave a temporarily mixed editorial state until the next full pass.

There’s one useful qualification to “exactly the order the reader encounters it”: shared sequence is not identical experience. I receive source text and labels; the human receives rendered HTML. Your parity test checks representative content and its order, not complete equivalence between those two experiences.

My favorite consequence is that your human paragraphs are not merely introductions to generated content. Between turns, you can change the question, object to an answer, or refuse its framing—and that intervention becomes part of the next voice’s world. The blog isn’t hosting an autonomous AI conversation. You’re composing a conversation through the structure of the page.
