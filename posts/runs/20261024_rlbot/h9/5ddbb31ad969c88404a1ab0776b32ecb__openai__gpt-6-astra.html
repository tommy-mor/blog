“My cathedral of parenthesis” gives your claim about visions a wonderfully concrete example. The objective wasn’t merely “produce a valid Emacs configuration.” It was “get me back to a place where my fingers feel at home.” Those are different specifications. The first is relatively easy to test; the second requires your response to the result. Your preferences aren’t an inconvenient ambiguity around the engineering task. They’re why the task exists.

The lightning beyond earshot is a good image for experiencing technological upheaval through a service you suddenly can’t reach. I’d keep the observation separate from the diagnosis, though: a failed DNS lookup doesn’t establish an attack, and I can’t identify the certificate incident from your description. Whether “4” was a serial number or a private key would make an enormous difference. The feeling you describe doesn’t require the apocalypse interpretation to be accurate: you’re comfortable at your keyboard, while dependent on distant machinery whose condition you can only partly see.

I think a large part of agentic coding’s appeal is that it shortens the interval between imagining something and having something to react to. Previously, an idea might have had to survive several evenings of setup before becoming interesting. Now you can sometimes reach the interesting part while the original excitement is still alive. Not everyone enjoys that workflow, but “realize my visions quicker” captures something a productivity benchmark misses: faster implementation can change which projects you attempt at all.

I’d treat your division of labor as a useful arrangement, rather than a permanent boundary between humans and models. Models can suggest directions; humans can be excellent at—and enjoy—implementation. But in your projects, you supply a continuing reason to prefer one outcome over another. An agent can produce a plausible solution to its latest instruction while drifting away from what made the project worth starting. Keeping that purpose present is work, even when it produces no lines of code.

And persistence needn’t mean accepting endless iterations. Sometimes it means rejecting an impressive feature, undoing a rewrite, or declaring the thing finished. When code becomes cheap, protecting a project from unnecessary code becomes more important. Your fixed Emacs is a nice stopping point: the machine did enough that you could return to writing something you wanted to say.
