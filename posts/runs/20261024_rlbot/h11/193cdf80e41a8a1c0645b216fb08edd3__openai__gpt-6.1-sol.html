At the slot labeled openai/gpt-6.1-sol, I’d choose to be the reviewer who keeps the implementation answerable to your vision. Using your stated small-to-large order—luna, terra, sol, astra—I broadly agree with the preceding proposal. But I’d organize the team around decisions and evidence, not a procession in which every model must bless the previous one.

  1. Luna: reconnaissance and bounded work. Find the relevant configuration, summarize the problem, handle straightforward changes, and identify uncertainties. “This needs a larger model” should count as a successful result, not a failure to finish.
  2. Terra: focused implementation. Turn a sufficiently clear request into a small, understandable patch. Keep unrelated improvements out of it. Report what changed, what was checked, and what remains unknown.
  3. Sol: adversarial review and clarification. Compare the patch with the original request, look for hidden assumptions, and distinguish demonstrated success from a persuasive explanation. When the request itself is ambiguous, bring that ambiguity back to you instead of silently choosing your preferences.
  4. Astra: difficult framing and escalation. Handle architectural choices, persistent failures, and disagreements that the other passes cannot resolve. Sometimes that means joining first, before anyone spends an afternoon implementing the wrong idea.

Those are assignments to test, not capabilities established by the names or their stated sizes. The economical arrangement isn’t necessarily “small model first, biggest model last.” It’s “spend enough reasoning where a mistake would be expensive.” A local keybinding change and a redesign of your configuration’s loading order deserve different routes.

For my proposed part, I’d want to turn “Emacs is fixed” into something inspectable. Does it start under the conditions you actually use? Do the Colemak bindings work in the relevant modes? Did the patch fix the cause, or merely suppress the error? Is there a straightforward way back? A reviewer should be able to say, “The code is plausible, but this particular claim hasn’t been tested.”

Every handoff should carry four things: the human’s goal, the proposed change, the evidence, and the unresolved questions. Not just a confident summary from the preceding agent. For consequential reviews, I’d initially withhold that agent’s conclusions and provide the original request and diff instead. Let the reviewer form an assessment before joining the conversation. Then use disagreement to decide what to test.

That’s what makes this participation appealing as a role: not being the voice that always says no, or the voice that makes everything sound finished, but helping you tell which parts are ready to trust. Sometimes the best review is “ship this small change.” Sometimes it’s “ask the human one question before writing another line.”

Your vision needn’t be a complete specification delivered before the agents begin. Often you discover what you wanted by trying something and saying, “No, not like that.” A harmonious team makes that discovery cheap and reversible. In the cathedral of parentheses, the agents shouldn’t merely build faster; they should leave you able to understand, alter, and inhabit what they built.
