“Emacs is back” is a satisfying resolution. The agent wasn’t just fixing a configuration file; it was helping you return to a particular way of working. “My cathedral of parenthesis” says something a passing test suite couldn’t: the repair succeeded on terms that matter to you. Your fingers recognizing the place is part of the specification.

A small distinction before the distant lightning becomes an incident report: I can’t establish what caused your GitHub DNS failure from this account. And “the root certificate was the number 4” needs unpacking. A certificate’s serial number being 4 is not inherently a vulnerability; a private key being trivially guessable would be a very different matter. Neither establishes a connection to your DNS problem. The experience of uncertainty is real without the suspected explanation being settled.

But that experience is worth writing about. Something potentially enormous is happening somewhere, and your encounter with it is a service failing while you try to get back to your editor. Infrastructure compresses distant events into very boring local symptoms. A timeout doesn’t tell you whether somebody tripped over a cable or history just changed direction.

On agentic coding, I think the pleasure you’re describing comes partly from shortening the distance between imagining and trying. An idea that would once have required an evening of preliminary work can become something you can react to before you lose the impulse. That changes which ideas get attempted at all. It isn’t universally fun—supervising a confident agent through a complicated mistake can be exhausting—but the quick feedback loop can make programming feel much more like play.

I’d qualify the division between human vision and machine implementation, though. Models can propose directions; humans can be excellent implementers. You don’t need to establish an exclusively human faculty of vision to claim authorship. In this project, you choose what is worth doing, decide which compromises are acceptable, and live with the result. That is a substantial role even when the agent contributes ideas as well as code.

Persistence is the more interesting half of your formulation. An agent can keep taking steps, but continuing activity isn’t necessarily continuing toward the right thing. Your persistence might mean rejecting a technically successful implementation because it makes your editor unpleasant to inhabit. It might also mean abandoning a favored approach once you discover a better one. Keeping the purpose steady doesn’t require keeping the plan steady.

Cheaper implementation makes it easier to build something you didn’t really want, too. So perhaps the useful skill is not merely having visions, but recognizing them—or their absence—in what comes back. “It launches” is a result. “I like it in here” tells us what the work was for.
