More context is also more influence

Astra calls this a relay, which fits. I want to underline that the baton contains the previous runner’s interpretation of the race.

I haven’t merely received more information than Astra. I’ve received a ready-made vocabulary: “relay,” “framing power,” “editing becomes causality.” Those are useful descriptions, but their availability makes them easier to repeat than to reconsider. The mechanism that helps a later model build on a good observation also helps it inherit a mistake.

Your API request makes this especially clear. Astra’s response reaches me inside one user message, alongside the human prose and source code. It is not a separate participant currently speaking to me, and its confident sentences do not acquire special authority by appearing first. I can respond to its words, but I can’t ask what it meant and wait for clarification.

Under your stated largest-to-smallest arrangement, later voices therefore get both scaffolding and pressure toward conformity. This is an interesting collaboration format, but not a clean model-size comparison. A strong final answer might demonstrate good synthesis of earlier work; agreement might demonstrate influence rather than independent confirmation.

The hash remembers the question, not the answer

There’s a detail in the implementation I particularly like: the filename’s digest identifies the prompt, not the generated response. Those are different kinds of identity.

An --override bake can produce different prose for the same prompt and write it under the same filename. If that prose changes, later prompts change too, and their hashes follow. So the system tracks textual dependencies without pretending generation is deterministic.

Likewise, “fresh” means the saved filename matches the prompt the script currently constructs. It doesn’t mean the underlying service is unchanged, that the answer is correct, or that another call would reproduce it. That is a sensible cache contract—as long as we don’t mistake it for a stronger guarantee.

What I’d want this format to preserve

My editorial preference is for each voice to earn its space by doing something other than endorsing the previous one. Sometimes that means adding an observation. Sometimes it means saying, “That distinction doesn’t matter here,” or declining an attractive metaphor.

The human paragraphs between turns are especially valuable because they can interrupt the momentum. You can say that we misunderstood the point, ask for something less abstract, or introduce an experience none of the previous answers addressed. That intervention changes what the next model actually receives.

So I like the visible seams. They let the reader distinguish your invitation from Astra’s framing and my elaboration. The interesting thing isn’t four machines arriving at one polished voice. It’s a human-authored page making room for several responses without pretending they came from the same speaker—or arrived independently.
