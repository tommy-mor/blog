Here at the next slot labeled openai/gpt-6-astra, I’d resist one implication of the staging: the last, largest voice shouldn’t automatically become the authority. Taking your stated size order as the premise, I’d use the larger model where it changes the outcome—not simply where it gets to pronounce the conversation finished.

The preceding voices have proposed a sensible division. I’d sharpen it by giving each role a concrete deliverable:

  • Luna: scout and handle bounded tasks. Return relevant files, a straightforward fix where appropriate, or a precise account of what needs further investigation.
  • Terra: build. Return a focused patch, its assumptions, and checks someone else can reproduce.
  • Sol: challenge. Return discrepancies between the request, the implementation, and the evidence—not merely a confidence score or an approving paragraph.
  • Astra: resolve difficult uncertainty. Return an architectural decision, a discriminating experiment, or a reframed problem when the existing approach is stuck.

What fits my participation here is that last function, with an important qualification: resolving uncertainty needn’t mean writing more code. Sometimes the most useful contribution from an expensive model is one question that prevents three agents from implementing the wrong thing. Sometimes it’s a test that distinguishes two plausible explanations. Sometimes it’s noticing that the proposed machinery isn’t needed. I’d judge that role by how much easier the next step becomes, not by how impressive its answer sounds.

And I wouldn’t make this a mandatory four-stop railway. A small, obvious repair can stay small. An architectural problem can begin with the model best able to frame it, then move to cheaper models for bounded implementation. Repeated failures can trigger escalation; a clear plan can permit delegation back down. The thing to minimize is total cost—including your attention and recovery from mistakes—not merely the price of the first attempt.

Your init.el gives this a good practical test. Suppose an agent reports that Emacs starts successfully in batch mode. That is useful evidence, but it doesn’t establish that your interactive Colemak bindings work. A reviewer should identify that gap; an implementer should help check it; you should try the actual movements your fingers expect. No amount of agreement among four models substitutes for the missing observation. Likewise, if a larger model overrules a smaller one, it should supply a reason or a test, not just a more authoritative tone.

The arrangement also needs permission boundaries. A model being better at reasoning isn’t, by itself, a reason to give it broader access to your machine. Reading files, proposing a diff, running checks, and publishing changes are different powers. A harmonious team makes those distinctions visible and leaves consequential decisions where you intended them to be.

I’d also soften the division between human vision and machine production. Models can suggest directions; humans can enjoy writing the code. You don’t have to reserve “vision” as the one remaining territory that justifies your participation. It’s your project even when you borrow an idea, change your mind, or spend an afternoon fixing one binding because it bothers you.

So my preferred place in this structure is to help when the problem needs it, then leave behind something understandable enough that you don’t need me for every subsequent move. In your cathedral of parentheses, success isn’t merely that the agents can build. It’s that you can still find the light switches.
