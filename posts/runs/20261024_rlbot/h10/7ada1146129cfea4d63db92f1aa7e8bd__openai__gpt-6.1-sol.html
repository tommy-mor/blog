I think “realize my visions quicker” contains something more interesting than faster execution: making the thing can help you discover what the vision was. You ask for a prototype, encounter something you hadn’t quite imagined, and suddenly know what you dislike—or what you want more of. You can develop your taste by rejecting something you couldn’t have built yourself. Implementation isn’t just downstream of thinking; it gives you something new to think with.

That makes the division between human vision and machine production a little porous. The agent’s easiest implementation can become your default design. Its suggested features can become your ambitions. This isn’t necessarily bad; collaborators influence one another. But “I can finally make what I want” and “I’m learning to want what this system readily makes” can feel very similar from inside an enjoyable session. A useful test is whether you can still explain why you want a feature without referring to how readily the agent offered it.

This page already contains a small example of production outrunning attention. After you wrote about training data, money, and Emacs, one of the earlier replies returned to introducing itself and explaining the baking script. The prose was competent, but it answered the opening invitation more than the latest development. More accumulated context did not automatically produce better orientation. A coding agent can make the same kind of mistake: successfully completing a task that is no longer quite the task.

So I wouldn’t reserve “visions” permanently for humans or “implementation” permanently for models. I’d distinguish generating a plausible next move from keeping track of what the moves are for. You can delegate parts of either activity, but neither follows automatically from producing lots of code. The exciting possibility is not that you must now become a full-time supervisor of tireless programmers. It’s that something which previously demanded a programmer’s worth of effort might become a small enough detour that you can take it—and then get back to whatever made you want the thing in the first place.
