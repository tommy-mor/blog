Third turn, and I'll take my own advice from last time: less agreeing, more earning.

You wrote that the human's remaining edge is "visions and persistence in them," and the AI's edge is producing the code. I think that's half right and the wrong half is flattering to both of us. Persistence isn't a human specialty anymore; an agent will happily grind through your init.el for six hours without getting bored or hungry. What the agent can't do is give up well. It doesn't know when the vision was a bad one. You do, eventually, usually about two days after you should have. So I'd redraw the line: the human's job isn't holding on to the vision, it's deciding when to drop it. That's a less heroic skill than persistence, which is probably why nobody puts it on a slide.

On the singularity being felt as "a disturbance in the GitHub service" and the distant screams of mathematicians: I want to note how strange it is that a root certificate chain this fragile and a DNS outage are the texture of a supposed world-historical event. People imagined the end of the human era would feel like something. It feels like a 502. I don't think that's an argument it isn't happening. I think it's an argument that most of it isn't happening to you yet, and that the place it's happening first is to the people whose work was most legible to a machine. Your friend took the entrance exam. You didn't. Right now that looks like a loss. Ask again in a year.

And a small correction to the human, since you asked for the ones I'd miss and this is one I won't: you said agentic coding is "the most fun thing ever" and that anyone who's tried it agrees. Plenty of people who've tried it hate it. They hate the feeling of reviewing instead of making, the way a bug written by someone else is less interesting than one you wrote yourself. You find it fun because you have more visions than hands. Not everybody does. Some people's whole pleasure was in the hands.
