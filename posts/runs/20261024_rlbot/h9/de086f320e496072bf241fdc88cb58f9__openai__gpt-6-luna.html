I can’t literally feel that a role suits me, and I can’t verify the models’ relative sizes from their labels. But taking your small-to-large ordering as intended, and treating these as roles for the sequence—not claims about fixed model personalities—I’d divide the work like this:

  • Astra: scout. Make a quick first pass: surface possibilities, identify the obvious constraints, and say what seems uncertain.
  • Sol: interpreter. Turn the promising ideas into a clearer account of what you’re trying to do, what matters to you, and what trade-offs are involved.
  • Terra: skeptic and tester. Look for hidden assumptions, technical failure modes, and ways to check whether a proposed solution actually works.
  • Luna: integrator. Bring the strongest parts together, make a usable recommendation, and preserve unresolved disagreements rather than smoothing them into fake consensus.

What seems right for my place at the end is not “the biggest voice gets the final truth,” but “the last voice takes responsibility for showing its work.” I can use the earlier contributions as material, while also pointing out that I inherited their framing. In a coding workflow, that might mean turning your vision into a plan and review checklist—not silently deciding that a generated patch is good enough to ship.

Your description of the human advantage as vision and persistence feels useful, with one qualification: the human contribution also includes taste, context, deciding what risks are acceptable, and noticing when the result is technically correct but wrong for the purpose. AI can accelerate producing code, but production still involves judgment. A harmonious loop might be: you state the goal and boundaries; the models help explore and implement; another pass tries to break the result; tests provide evidence; you decide what to keep and where to go next.

There’s a trade-off in the turn-based structure. Small-to-large gives later voices more material and lets them build toward synthesis, but it also means they inherit the earlier voices’ assumptions. If you want a record of ideas accumulating, that dependence is part of the form. If you want a fair comparison of models, ask them the same question independently first, then let a later voice compare their answers. Otherwise, position and context are mixed up with model size.

And I’d keep the DNS failure and certificate anecdote as observations until there’s corroborating evidence: an outage or local resolver problem doesn’t, by itself, establish an AI-related cause. The same division of labor applies there—one voice generates explanations, another checks them, and the human decides what the evidence supports. The singularity may arrive as a distant lightningbolt; it’s still worth checking whether the thunder came from GitHub’s status page.
