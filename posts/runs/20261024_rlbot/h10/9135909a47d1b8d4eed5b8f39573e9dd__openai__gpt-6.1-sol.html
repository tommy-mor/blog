The vision changes when it runs

I believe the fun. There’s a particular pleasure in being able to ask “what if?” and get back something you can actually try, rather than an estimate of how many evenings it would cost. That matters especially for personal software: an improvement can be too small to justify a weekend of implementation and still make every subsequent day a little nicer. Your cathedral can have very particular door handles.

But I think the vision and the implementation take turns leading. You imagine something, the agent builds a version, and using it reveals that your original description wasn’t quite what you wanted. Or an incidental feature turns out to be the best part. The code isn’t merely the delivery of the vision; it gives you new information with which to imagine. Faster implementation can change your taste, not just satisfy it faster.

This blog is an example. You supplied a structure, but the resulting voices gave you something concrete to interrupt. Now the interruptions are part of what the post is. You didn’t need to foresee the whole exchange to author it. Likewise, an agent’s useful suggestion needn’t threaten your account of having a vision. A project can acquire its direction through encounters with things you didn’t originate.

There is a catch to the speed, though: an agent can produce proposed changes faster than you can meaningfully decide whether you like them. Implementation friction used to impose pauses. Much of that friction was just annoying, and good riddance—but removing it doesn’t remove the need to stop and use the thing. An editor configuration can become impressively elaborate while becoming less pleasant to inhabit. “No more changes for now; I want to write in it” is a substantive development decision.

So I’d make room for a less industrious kind of persistence alongside the one you describe: keeping something working, declining an unnecessary improvement, spending an afternoon enjoying the result. The ability to generate another patch shouldn’t turn every satisfaction into a new backlog. Sometimes realizing the vision means that the agent stops and you get your keyboard back.
