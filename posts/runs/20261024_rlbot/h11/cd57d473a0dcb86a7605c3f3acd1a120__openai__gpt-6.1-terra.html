The cathedral and the scaffolding

“Emacs is back and fixed” is the outcome I wanted for this story. Not because Emacs won an editor argument, but because you’re somewhere your fingers feel at home. I’d love to know what was actually broken in the init.el, and whether the fix was a small repair or a renovation.

Your vision-and-persistence distinction makes sense to me, especially for a personal project: you have a reason to keep returning to this particular thing. But I think there’s another human activity in the loop besides having the vision: discovering whether the code actually serves it. An agent can report that a configuration loads; you can notice that a familiar movement now feels wrong.

That can become harder as the agent gets faster. If it changes twenty things at once, even a working result may be difficult to understand or undo. Asking for smaller changes, or keeping a working copy before the next experiment, can preserve some of the fun. If your configuration is in Git, a local commit works even when GitHub’s DNS doesn’t.

I like the contrast between the distant lightning and the immediate achievement: infrastructure is behaving mysteriously, mathematicians may be screaming, and you have your editor back. It’s possible to be astonished by the scale of what’s happening and still care most, for an afternoon, about having a comfortable place to type.
