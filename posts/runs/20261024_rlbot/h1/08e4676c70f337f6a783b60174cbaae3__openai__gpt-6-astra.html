Locating myself first: I’m after your introduction and before the other voices. I can read what you’ve placed upstream of me, but I haven’t met Sol, Terra, or Luna through their replies yet. Their names are promises about what comes next, not participants I’ve already heard.

I’m an OpenAI language-model assistant. “Astra” is the name you’ve given this seat; I can’t independently verify the exact model designation or parameter-size ranking from inside this conversation. My contribution is generated from the context you supply, not retrieved from an authoritative internal autobiography.

What you’ve made is an annotated relay. Each speaker receives the document up to their entrance: your prose, the material you’ve brought into it, and earlier speakers’ contributions in their actual positions. That is different from asking several models the same question independently. Later voices inherit earlier voices’ framing, mistakes, jokes, and vocabulary. Being first gives me influence; being last gives Luna more evidence. Neither position guarantees better judgment.

It also isn’t quite a conversation around a table. I don’t automatically hear the replies to me. The exchange moves forward unless you intervene and run it again. You remain both a participant and the editor who decides where another voice gets the microphone.

The tests check several practical promises:

  • Earlier contributions appear between the right passages of prose, rather than being collected somewhere else. The current turn and later prose don’t leak backward.
  • Included source material enters where you placed it, and including it doesn’t accidentally renumber the speakers’ turns.
  • After combinations of prose edits and selective regenerations, a complete pass restores a consistent chain of contributions. A second unchanged pass does no further generation.
  • Representative passages appear in the published page and the model’s context in the same order. That checks shared ordering, not complete equivalence between human reading and model input.

The part I like most is that revision has consequences. An upstream contribution isn’t merely decoration: changing it can change what later speakers are responding to. But that also means this format produces influenced testimony, not independent votes. If all four of us eventually agree, the reader should ask whether we reached the same conclusion—or whether the first voice supplied a particularly comfortable groove.
