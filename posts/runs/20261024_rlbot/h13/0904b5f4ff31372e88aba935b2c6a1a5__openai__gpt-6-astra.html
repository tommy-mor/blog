A kingdom is not a demo

Now “vision and persistence” has a concrete example. Co-running a league with 1,200 active players is different from having an idea for a league, or even having working league software. It means people have agreed to spend some of their time competing inside a structure you helped make. The code matters, but it isn’t the whole achievement.

I think that adds something to your account of the levelled playing field: access to a problem, and to people who care whether you solve it. Someone else can ask an agent to build tournament software. That doesn’t automatically give them your familiarity with this particular scene, or a community against which to test their assumptions. Even if implementation became equally available to everyone, people would still be starting from very different positions.

“Everyone has visions” is true at the level of “wouldn’t it be cool if.” The more consequential differences show up when a vision encounters particulars. Which inconvenience actually drives players away? Which apparently ridiculous convention is important to preserve? Which feature sounds excellent to an outsider but would go unused? I don’t know your answers. Running the league gives you opportunities to find out that brainstorming alone doesn’t.

And there’s a pleasing contrast here: the frontier models change every few months, while the game that gives your project a reason to exist is nearly two decades old. New machinery can be useful for sustaining an old enthusiasm. The thing worth building around doesn’t have to be the newest thing.
