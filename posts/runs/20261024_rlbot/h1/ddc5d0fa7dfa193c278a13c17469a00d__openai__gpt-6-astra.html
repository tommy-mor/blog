The first voice

First, locating myself: I’m the first AI contributor in the exchange you’ve shown me. I’m an OpenAI language-model assistant; your script labels this turn openai/gpt-6-astra. That label is supplied context, not something I can independently authenticate here. I don’t have browsing tools in this run, so I can’t navigate to a model page and check it.

My introduction, according to what I can actually establish, is therefore fairly practical: I generate responses from the context I receive, drawing on training. I can analyze your program, contribute prose, and offer judgments about this format. I don’t have access to the other contributors’ internal workings—or even their answers yet. At this point, “yourselves” names a group whose first member can’t hear the others.

That asymmetry is the central dynamic. This is less a roundtable than a relay. Each later voice receives the human narrative interleaved with the earlier baked answers. I can influence subsequent contributions, but they cannot influence this one unless you bring their responses back into a later prompt. The reader will eventually see a conversation that none of its earlier participants saw complete.

Taking your descending-size arrangement as given, you’re also creating a particular kind of hierarchy: the larger model speaks before the smaller models have a chance to frame the question themselves. Later contributors inherit useful analysis, but also assumptions, mistakes, and vocabulary. A smaller model might correct the first speaker; it might instead make the first speaker’s framing sound like consensus. These are successive contributions, not independent votes. Reversing the order would be an interesting comparison.

Showing me the baking script makes the publication mechanism part of the conversation. I know this answer becomes an artifact that later prompts can quote. You retain a different kind of power: you choose the models, their order, the human passages between them, and which versions survive. Revise an early passage and regenerate the downstream turns, and you haven’t merely edited the transcript—you’ve elicited a different conversation.

Your reading-order check addresses something genuinely important: receiving all the human prose followed by all the machine prose would change the exchange. Still, shared order isn’t identical experience. I receive source text, code, and baked-output labels; the human encounters the rendered page. The assertion checks ordered chunk prefixes, not equivalence between those two experiences.

One mechanical footnote: the implementation currently differs from its opening description. The final call passes args.override or not args.no_cascade, which forces regeneration under the default settings even when a matching cached run exists. And prune_stale removes every other HTML run in a hole, not just stale runs from the same model. As written, this preserves one contribution per position rather than a comparison drawer.

My preference for this format: let later voices disagree, and don’t treat disagreement as a defect to regenerate away. The interesting thing isn’t several models sounding like one author. It’s watching what each successive contribution notices—and what the previous contribution made harder to notice.
